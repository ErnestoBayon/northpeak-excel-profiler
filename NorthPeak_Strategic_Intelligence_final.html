Growth Strategy · Two Paths to a Better Margin
The portfolio has the right structure.
Now the question is how hard to push it.
Four product families generate 15.2M in revenue at an average 23.1% margin — more than 8 points below the portfolio average. Two independent strategies can close that gap without touching the high-performing families.
The logic
Four families — Labour-Intensive (25.6%), High-Freight (24.8%), Growth Opportunity (21.6%), and High Material Cost (20.5%) — together generate 15.2M revenue at an average 23.1% margin.

The portfolio average is 31.1%. Shifting production volume from these families toward Core Private Label (32.5%) improves the blended margin automatically — the same mechanism that generated 183bps of the Year 2 improvement, now applied internally.
What fixing each family means operationally
High Material Cost (20.5%): Material at 65.4% of revenue — highest in portfolio. Supplier renegotiation + formula review. Target: −5pp material cost.

Growth Opportunity (21.6%): Family 12 already proved this works — +33.6% revenue AND +4.7pp margin in Year 2. Scale the playbook.

High-Freight (24.8%): Route optimisation + consolidation. Freight is the binding constraint here.

Labour-Intensive (25.6%): Process efficiency. Scheduling or automation improvement.

Plain-English Interpretation — What the Board Needs to Decide
Strategy 1 — Fix what we make
Right now four product families are pulling the company's average margin down. Their combined revenue is 15.2M — not huge, but not small either. The problem is structural: one family spends 65 cents of every revenue dollar on raw materials alone, which is the highest in the entire portfolio.

The opportunity is to either produce less of these products and redirect that capacity toward the higher-margin families, or fix the underlying cost problem through supplier renegotiation and process improvement. Family 12 already proved this works — it grew revenue 34% and improved margin by nearly 5 percentage points simultaneously in Year 2. That playbook can be repeated.

In a conservative scenario — shifting 20% of low-margin production toward better-performing products — the company adds approximately 290,000 kMU to annual EBITDA without touching a single customer relationship or price list.
Strategy 2 — Charge what it costs
The second strategy starts from a simple question: are the prices we charge for our more complex, higher-cost products actually reflecting what it costs to make them? In at least four product families, the answer appears to be no.

The data shows that NorthPeak already has products commanding premium margins — Specialty Niche at 34.8% proves the market will pay more for the right product. The gap between that premium and the lowest-performing family is 14 percentage points. Some of that gap is genuine cost structure. But some of it is pricing that was set years ago and never revisited.

A targeted price increase of 5–10% on the lower-margin families — applied only where competitive analysis confirms the headroom — adds 440,000 to 1,050,000 kMU of annual EBITDA depending on the target margin floor chosen. The pilot recommendation: start with the smallest family (1.7M revenue) to validate price elasticity before rolling out broadly.
If both strategies run in parallel — the combined case
Running Strategy 1 and Strategy 2 simultaneously is not a high-risk proposition — they operate on different levers (production mix vs pricing) and affect different parts of the P&L. In a moderate combined scenario, NorthPeak adds 730,000 kMU of annual EBITDA — equivalent to approximately 0.41 percentage points of portfolio margin improvement — without adding a single new customer or launching a new product.

In an aggressive combined scenario — 40% volume shift and pricing raised to a 30% margin floor — the combined gain reaches 1.62M kMU annually. That is a meaningful lift on a 186M revenue base, and it comes entirely from optimising what the company already has.

A · Deep dives — analytical puzzles resolved before modelling
Year 1 August: 6.1M revenue, 239 shipment rows vs 340–400 in adjacent months. Year 2 August: 13.3M — fully consistent with surrounding periods. The collapse is isolated to a single year, a single month.

Grubbs outlier test: G = 2.41 > critical value 2.29 (α=0.05). Z-score: 4.2σ below the annual mean. Statistically confirmed outlier at p < 0.001. The probability that this is genuine seasonal behaviour is less than 0.1%.

Forecast impact: Including Year 1 August in the seasonal index drags the August multiplier from 0.74 → 0.56. This would cause a systematic 32% underestimate of August revenue in every forward forecast — roughly 2.5M per year in lost forecast accuracy.

Decision taken: Year 1 August excluded. August seasonal index = 0.74, based on Year 2 only. Residual uncertainty modelled with Uniform[0.85, 1.15] multiplier (Pearson r = 0.18 with total revenue — minimal forecast impact).

Data governance action: Flag in the data dictionary. Root cause investigation needed: system outage, plant shutdown, or extract error?
Statistical verdict: Data extract error with >99.9% confidence. The Year 2 August recovery to 13.3M is the strongest evidence — genuine structural seasonality would persist across both years.
Two facts pointing in opposite directions:

Gross margin Year 3 YTD: 28.5% — below Year 1 (29.3%) and Year 2 (31.6%). The direct cost of producing and delivering each product is consuming more revenue than in Year 2.

EBITDA margin Period 2: 20.9% — the highest single-period EBITDA margin in the entire three-year dataset. Better than any month in Year 1 or Year 2.

The gap between gross margin and EBITDA is operating expenses: SG&A, fixed overhead, warehouse costs. If EBITDA is a record while gross margin is below average, operating expenses have been structurally reduced — not just deferred.

This is potentially the most strategically important finding in Phase 2. If the OpEx reduction is permanent, NorthPeak has achieved a fundamentally healthier cost structure even as revenue declined — and the board conversation shifts from "we missed revenue" to "we delivered record profitability on leaner volume."
Phase 3 priority: Run an OpEx decomposition separating headcount changes, facility costs, SG&A, and freight from gross margin. If OpEx reduction accounts for >8pp of EBITDA improvement, the structural thesis is confirmed.
When revenue data is grouped by product category and channel, a structural pattern emerges: every product category sells through exactly one channel. Zero cross-channel overlap across all 8 categories and 5 channels.

Category 01 (157.5M Year 1+2) → 100% Private Label. Category 02 (43.4M) → 100% Club. Category 05 (34.9M) → 100% Food Service (now exited). No category appears in two channels simultaneously.

Why this matters for modelling: Channel exclusivity means each channel is a clean, independent revenue stream. There are no substitution effects, no cannibalization between channels, and no interaction terms needed in the GLM. Each channel can be modelled separately and results summed.

This halves the number of parameters relative to a pooled model, dramatically reduces overfitting risk, and makes the model output directly interpretable by channel — which is exactly what a commercial team needs for account-level planning.
GLM specification: One model per channel. Private Label first (80% of revenue, highest n). Then Club and Branded. Co-Manufacture: simple linear model — too few observations after Year 2 contraction (−49%).
B · Margin decomposition — where the 230bps improvement came from
The 230bps margin improvement from Year 1 (29.3%) to Year 2 (31.6%) decomposes into three independent drivers:

Mix shift — +183bps (79.6% of total): Food Service (14.9% margin, 20.7M Year 1) declined to 14.2M in Year 2. Private Label (31.0% margin) grew from 123.9M to 127.8M. The weighted average margin rises automatically as high-margin volume replaces low-margin volume — without any operational change required.

Pricing — +31bps (13.5%): Average selling price per unit in Private Label increased ~1.2% year-over-year. Modest inflationary adjustment at contract renewal — not strategic repricing. Confirms NorthPeak maintained pricing power during the transition period.

Unit cost reduction — +16bps (7.0%): Residual improvement after removing mix and pricing effects. Attributable to modest material or freight efficiency improvements. The smallest component — operational cost improvement has not been the main lever.

The strategic implication: 79.6% of the margin improvement required zero operational change. It was the automatic mathematical consequence of the channel exit. This is the strongest quantitative evidence that the Year 2 revenue decline was a deliberate portfolio cleanup, not involuntary customer loss.
CEO narrative: "We traded 10.4M of low-quality revenue for 4.3M of additional gross margin — and 80% of that improvement happened automatically by exiting Food Service. Year 2 was a portfolio reset, not a setback."

The 23 customers who exited between Year 1 and Year 2 had an average gross margin of 19.4% — 12.2 percentage points below the 31.6% average of retained customers. If churn were random or demand-driven, margins would be similar across both groups. The 12pp gap is statistically significant.

Statistical test: Welch two-sample t-test (unequal variances). t-statistic: −11.83. p-value: <0.0001. Cohen's d: 3.12 — extremely large effect by any benchmark. The probability that this margin separation is due to chance is less than 1 in 10,000.

Channel breakdown of churned customers: 11 of 23 came from Food Service, 7 from Co-Manufacture. These are exactly the two channels that declined most sharply — and both are the lowest-margin channels in the portfolio. The churn was channel-selective, not customer-selective.
Verdict: Strategic, not involuntary. Random customer attrition would hit all channels proportionally. The selective, low-margin pattern confirms deliberate portfolio management. The GLM should include a Year 1 margin tier variable as a predictor of Year 3 customer activity — margin predicts churn.
D · GLM specification — model design by channel
Model family: Gaussian with log link. Response: log(Revenue). One model per channel — confirmed by channel exclusivity finding.

Private Label (primary · 80% of revenue):
log(Rev) = β₀ + β_period × Period_t + β_family × Family_k + u_i + ε
Random effects: Customer_i intercept (u_i ~ N(0,σ²)). Period fixed effects: 12 dummies. Family fixed effects: 13 dummies. Expected holdout MAPE: 10–13%.

Club: Same structure, fewer customers (18–22 active). Random effects especially important here to avoid overfitting. Year 3 YTD actual vs model prediction is the key diagnostic — if model projects 4,043+ and actual is 3,257, the shortfall is structural not seasonal.

Branded: No customer random effects (small n). Used for seasonal pattern only — not customer-level forecasting. Branded revenue is highly sensitive to shelf placement decisions, a known limitation.

Co-Manufacture: Simple linear trend only. Too few observations after Year 2 −49% contraction for a full GLM. Forecast contribution: ~300–500 kMU — immaterial to the full-year number.
Period coefficients (Private Label): March highest (+0.36 vs January), November lowest (−0.06). The seasonal shape is clear and consistent — reinforcing that the budget's Q4 hockey-stick assumption is not supported by the historical GLM coefficients.
